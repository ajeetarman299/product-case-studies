# SportsHedge: shaping a cricket player-trading MVP

Product Management Intern · May to Jul 2024 · Remote

| Interviews | Platforms benchmarked | Influencer partners | Visitors in 2 months |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30+ | 7 | 10+ | 46k+ |

## Context

SportsHedge was building a platform where cricket fans trade players. I joined as a product management intern for three months, working remotely. The product was still being shaped, so my work covered the early path end to end: research, scope, design and launch.

## The problem

The team had two open questions:

1. **What should the first version do?** A trading product can grow a long feature list fast. The team needed a scope small enough to build and launch quickly, but strong enough that fans would choose it over what they already used.
2. **How do we get the first users?** A new platform with no audience needs a launch plan that brings in real cricket fans, not just a website.

## The users

Cricket fans, including fans trying a trading product for the first time. That set the bar for the design: a first-time user had to be able to understand and complete a trade without help.

## Research

**30+ user interviews.** I synthesized more than 30 user interviews. Rather than keeping impressions from each one, I used sentiment analysis to group what people said and turn their complaints into a single, ranked list of pain points.

**7 platforms benchmarked.** I compared seven sports-trading platforms feature by feature. The comparison sorted features into two groups:

- **Table stakes:** what every platform offered and users would expect on day one.
- **Differentiators:** places where the existing platforms were weak and SportsHedge could stand apart.

Put together, the two pieces of research answered different questions. The interviews said what hurt. The benchmark said what users already took for granted and where the gaps were.

## Options considered

There were two ways to scope the first version:

- **Match the market.** Build everything the benchmarked platforms had, then add something new later. This looks safe, but it delays launch and gives fans no reason to switch.
- **Narrow and sharp.** Ship the table stakes so the product feels complete, put the build effort into the differentiators, and cut everything else.

We went with the second. An MVP's job is to learn whether fans will use the product, and a copy of existing platforms would not teach us that.

## What made the MVP, and what was cut

Every pain point from the interviews became a candidate feature. I ranked candidates by user pain first and effort second, then used the benchmark to draw the MVP line:

- **In:** the table stakes, because a trading product that misses basics loses trust on the first visit.
- **In:** the differentiators that matched the highest-ranked pain points, because that is where SportsHedge had a reason to exist.
- **Cut, for now:** anything that was neither a table stake nor tied to a top pain point. Those ideas could wait for real usage data.

The ranking meant every scope call had a reason the team could see.

## Design

I designed the wireframes and user flows in Figma. The goal was an experience with no ambiguous steps: a fan who had never traded should know at every screen what they own and what happens when they tap.

## Go-to-market

Cricket fans already follow creators who talk about the game, so the launch went through them. I led negotiations with more than 10 influencers and ran data-led marketing campaigns alongside the partnerships.

## Result

- **46k+ visitors** to the site in the first 2 months.
- **10+ influencer partnerships** set up and run.
- An MVP scope and a set of Figma flows the team could build from, with a clear reason behind each feature.

## What I would do differently

- **Measure activation, not just reach.** 46k+ visitors shows the launch reached people. It does not show how many made a first trade. Next time I would define activation before launch and track the funnel from visit to sign-up to first trade.
- **Keep the scoring visible.** I ranked features by pain and effort, but a simple shared scoring sheet would have let the team challenge and audit each ranking.
- **Interview again after launch.** The 30+ interviews shaped the scope. A second, smaller round with real users after launch would show whether the differentiators actually landed.
